## What Amazon the company is

Amazon was founded in 1994 in the United States as an online bookstore. It is now one of the largest retailers in the world. People often think "Amazon" means the website where they buy products, but the company actually runs four or five separate businesses at the same time:

- The Amazon marketplace, the website shoppers visit to buy products.
- Amazon Web Services (AWS), the cloud-computing platform that hosts a large share of the public internet. You are not running ads on AWS.
- Prime Video, Twitch, Kindle, Ring, and other consumer products. You are not running ads on these either.
- The advertising business, the part you will spend your time on.

This course covers only the marketplace and the advertising business. When the curriculum says "Amazon," it means the marketplace and the ads platform, not the rest of the company.

## Who shops on Amazon

Hundreds of millions of shoppers buy on Amazon each year. Some buy every week. Others buy once a year. They use Amazon because:

- Search works. A shopper typing "bamboo cutting board" sees relevant results at the top.
- Delivery is fast, often one or two days with Prime.
- Returns are easy. A buyer can send most items back within 30 days for a full refund.
- Trust is high. Reviews, ratings, and the "buy box" (the listing position a shopper can add to cart from) make it hard to get tricked by a bad listing.

A sponsored ad does not change any of that. The ad directs a shopper to a product page. From there, the shopper decides whether to buy based on the listing itself.

## Who sells on Amazon

Most products on Amazon are not sold by Amazon itself. They are sold by independent third-party sellers. There are three main seller types you will encounter:

- Third-party seller (3P). An independent business or individual who lists products on Amazon and uses Amazon to handle payment and (often) shipping. Most PPC work is for 3P sellers.
- First-party vendor (1P). A brand or manufacturer that sells wholesale to Amazon. Amazon then resells the product. 1P sellers have less direct control over their ads.
- Brand-owner seller. A manufacturer who sells directly to shoppers on Amazon as a 3P seller. Many brands run as brand owners to keep full control of pricing and ads.

Your client is almost certainly a third-party seller. They pay Amazon a subscription fee, a referral fee on each sale, and (if they use FBA) a fulfillment fee.

## How Amazon makes money from a single product

Take a ₱1,000 bamboo cutting board sold by a 3P seller using FBA. When a shopper buys it, Amazon collects ₱1,000 and keeps several slices:

- Referral fee: 15% of the sale price for most categories, so ₱150. The category matters; some categories are lower or higher.
- FBA fee: a per-unit fee Amazon charges for picking, packing, and shipping. The exact fee depends on the product's size and weight. A cutting board ships as a large standard-size item, so expect roughly ₱200 to ₱300.
- Optional fees: storage fees for slow-moving inventory, advertising fees (the slice you control), returns processing if the buyer sends it back.

After all of that, the seller is left with the remainder, called the contribution margin. That margin has to cover the seller's product cost (what they paid the manufacturer), shipping to Amazon's warehouse, and any profit.

The key insight: Amazon takes its fees before the seller takes profit. The seller cannot lower the referral fee or the FBA fee by running better ads. Ads are paid separately and live on top of the seller's contribution margin.

## Why ads exist on Amazon at all

Without ads, a shopper searching for "bamboo cutting board" sees only organic results. Amazon's search algorithm ranks those results based on relevance, sales history, and other signals. The seller cannot pay to influence the algorithm directly.

Ads change that. A seller can pay to appear above or beside the organic results. They pay per click, not per showing. The shopper still sees a "Sponsored" label, so ads do not pretend to be organic. But they let a seller put a product in front of a shopper who would not otherwise have found it.

For a new product with no sales history, ads are how the product gets initial visibility. For an established product, ads defend market share against competitors who are running their own ads. For a slow-moving product, ads can clear inventory before storage fees eat the margin.

Every campaign you will ever run lives inside this system: a seller pays Amazon to show a product to a shopper, hoping the shopper buys it at a margin that covers the click cost.

## Where you fit in

You are the person the seller hires to run the ads. You do not invent the seller's business. You do not decide the price, the product, or the marketplace. You decide, within the rules Amazon sets:

- Which search terms trigger the ad.
- How much the seller is willing to pay per click.
- How the daily budget is allocated across campaigns.
- When to pause, raise, lower, or leave a campaign alone.
- How to explain each change to the seller.

The rest of this course teaches you how to make those decisions safely.

## Quick check

1. Which type of seller is most PPC work for: third-party sellers, first-party vendors, or AWS customers?
2. What two fees does Amazon typically take on every sale for a 3P seller using FBA?
3. What does a shopper pay when they click a Sponsored Products ad?

**Answers:** (1) Third-party sellers. AWS is unrelated to marketplace ads. (2) The referral fee (a percentage of the sale price) and the FBA fee (a per-unit fee for fulfillment). (3) Nothing. The shopper pays nothing extra. The seller pays the click cost from their ad budget.

## Key takeaway

Amazon is a marketplace with hundreds of millions of shoppers and millions of third-party sellers. The ads platform exists because Amazon lets sellers pay to appear in front of shoppers who would not otherwise have seen the product. Your job as a PPC VA is to spend that ad money well.
