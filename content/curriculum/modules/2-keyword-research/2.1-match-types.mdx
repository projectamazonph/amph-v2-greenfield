## What you can do after this lesson

Choose the right match type, broad, phrase, or exact, for a keyword's role in a campaign, and explain that choice to a client in plain terms.

## The decision in one sentence

Match type controls how loosely a shopper's search has to match your keyword before your ad can show. Use broad to discover, phrase to scale, exact to protect proven winners.

When you add a keyword to a campaign, you are not just telling Amazon "show my ad for this exact word." You are choosing how closely a shopper's search term has to match your keyword before your ad appears. That choice is your match type, and it is one of the most powerful levers you have.

The three match types are broad, phrase, and exact. Each one trades reach for control. We'll go through each one, then put them together in a hierarchy.

---

## Broad Match (Discovery)

Broad match is the most permissive option. Your ad can show for any search term that Amazon's algorithm considers related to your keyword, even if the words are different.

**What it does:** Amazon analyzes your keyword, understands the intent behind it, and matches it to related searches.

**Example:** Your keyword is `water bottle`:

| Match Type | Search Term That Triggers | Does It Match? |
|------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| Broad | "water bottle" | Yes |
| Broad | "stainless steel water bottle" | Yes |
| Broad | "large water bottle for gym" | Yes |
| Broad | "glass cup" | Possibly |
| Broad | "phone case" | No |

**When to use it:**
- Discovery phase. Launching a new product and you don't know which search terms convert.
- Auto-campaign alternative. When you want Amazon's algorithm to find new keyword opportunities.
- Low-competition niches. Where irrelevant clicks are cheap.

**When to pause and review:**
- Tight budgets. A broad match without a negative list will spend quickly.
- Mature campaigns. Once you know your winning keywords, broad match wastes impressions.
- Low tolerance for high ACoS (advertising cost of sales). Broad match always has the highest wasted spend.

Pair broad match with a negative keyword list. Negatives are the filter that lets broad match stay useful without bleeding spend.

---

## Phrase Match (Scaling)

Phrase match sits between broad and exact. Your ad shows when the shopper's search term contains your exact keyword phrase in the same order, with possible additional words before or after.

**What it does:** The shopper must include your exact phrase. They can add words on either side.

**Example:** Your keyword is `"water bottle"` (phrase match):

| Search Term | Matches? | Why |
|-------------|----------|-----|
| "water bottle" | Yes | Your exact phrase |
| "stainless steel water bottle" | Yes | Words before |
| "water bottle for gym" | Yes | Words after |
| "large water bottle 32oz" | Yes | Words before and after |
| "bottle of water" | No | Wrong order |
| "water bottles" | Possibly | Close variants |

**When to use it:**
- Balanced approach. More traffic than exact, more control than broad.
- Modifier variations. Find long-tail variations of your core keywords.
- Scale phase. After discovering winning terms through broad, tighten to phrase.

**One critical detail:** Amazon updated phrase match in 2022. It now includes close variants (plurals, misspellings, abbreviations). "Water bottle" phrase match may also match "water bottles" or "waterbottle." This is usually helpful and worth knowing.

Phrase match is often the most efficient match type for scaling. It captures the long-tail traffic that exact misses while filtering out the junk that broad lets through.

---

## Exact Match (Protection)

Exact match is the most restrictive. Your ad shows only when the shopper's search term matches your keyword exactly, including Amazon's close variants.

**What it does:** Maximum control. You decide which search terms trigger your ad.

**Example:** Your keyword is `[water bottle]` (exact match):

| Search Term | Matches? | Why |
|-------------|----------|-----|
| "water bottle" | Yes | Exact match |
| "water bottles" | Yes | Close variant |
| "waterbottle" | Yes | Close variant |
| "water bottel" | Yes | Close variant (misspelling) |
| "stainless steel water bottle" | No | Extra words |
| "water bottle 32oz" | No | Extra word after |

**When to use it:**
- Proven converters. Keywords you know drive sales at a good ACoS.
- High-volume head terms. Where broad match would waste thousands on irrelevant clicks.
- Profit protection. When you need to guarantee your ad only shows for specific searches.

**When to pause and review:**
- Discovery. You will miss long-tail variations that could convert.
- New campaigns. You need data before you know which exact terms work.
- Low volume. Exact match limits your reach significantly.

A common workflow: start with broad or phrase, let them run for two to four weeks, then mine the search term report for high-converting terms and move those into exact match campaigns. This is called the harvesting strategy, and it is a core pattern in Module 7.

## The Hierarchy Strategy

Here is how the three match types work together in a mature account:

```
CAMPAIGN: Broad Discovery
  Ad Group: Broad Match
    Keywords: water bottle (broad)

CAMPAIGN: Phrase Scaling
  Ad Group: Phrase Match
    Keywords: "water bottle" (phrase)

CAMPAIGN: Exact Profit
  Ad Group: Exact Match
    Keywords: [water bottle] (exact)
```

The flow: broad catches everything, including irrelevant terms, so you add negatives to filter them out. Phrase catches the valuable long-tail that broad discovered. Exact captures your proven winners for maximum efficiency.

**Key takeaways:**
- Broad is the discovery engine. Expensive but essential for finding new keywords.
- Phrase is the balanced middle. Best for scaling what you have learned.
- Exact is the profit zone. Highest conversion, lowest waste, highest CPC.
- Run all three in separate campaigns for proper budget control.
- The hierarchy works downward: broad → phrase → exact.
- Add negatives every week, in every campaign.

## Your turn

Work this through before checking the answer.

You've been running one broad-match ad group for a $45 stainless-steel water bottle for three weeks. The search term report shows "stainless steel water bottle" converting at 14% with a $1.10 CPC, and "water bottle" alone converting at 1.5% with a $0.95 CPC and no sign of improving.

Before reading further: what do you do with each of these two search terms, and why?

**Work it through:** "Stainless steel water bottle" is proven and specific. Move it to its own exact-match campaign so you can bid it up without paying broad-match rates for a term you already know converts. "Water bottle" alone is broad, low-converting, and has had three weeks to prove itself; it hasn't. Add it as a negative in the broad campaign (exact or phrase, depending on how much of that broader traffic is worth keeping) so the broad-match budget goes to terms still worth discovering, not one that's already shown its hand.

## Grouping decision

Before you move on, write three keywords for one product and assign each a match type. For each assignment, state the shopper intent you are protecting and one reason the other match types would be less safe.

## Client language

Use this when a client asks why you're splitting one campaign into three:

> "I'm separating your keywords by match type so each one gets a bid that matches how confident we are it'll convert. Proven terms get their own campaign so we can raise bids safely, while new terms stay in a broader net so we keep discovering without overpaying."

## Quick check

1. Your keyword is broad-match "water bottle." Would "large water bottle for gym" trigger your ad?
2. Your keyword is phrase-match "water bottle." Would "bottle of water" trigger your ad? Why or why not?
3. Which match type has the lowest wasted spend but also the lowest reach?
4. What's the "harvesting strategy"?

**Answers:** (1) Yes, broad match reads it as related intent even though the wording differs. (2) No, phrase match requires your exact phrase in the same order, and "bottle of water" reverses the word order. (3) Exact match. (4) Let broad or phrase match run for 2-4 weeks, mine the search term report for high-converting terms, then move those proven terms into exact match campaigns.
